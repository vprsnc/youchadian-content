# The Four Famous Jades of China (四大名玉) — and the 'King' from Abroad, Burmese Jadeite

## Introduction

"Sì dà míng yù" (四大名玉), the four famous jades of China, is not an ancient canonical concept. The list found in today's books and museums **was assembled after 1949**. It contains only four stones mined within China's borders: 和田玉 hétián yù, 独山玉 dúshān yù, 岫玉 xiù yù, and 蓝田玉 lántián yù. No imperial decree or classical treatise confirms this quartet. However, the market features another stone — Burmese jadeite 缅甸翡翠 miǎndiàn fěicuì — which is not part of the "four jades" (all of which are Chinese), yet is called the "king of jades" 玉中之王 yù zhōng zhī wáng by Chinese connoisseurs.

Let us examine them in order.

## 四大名玉 Sì Dà Míng Yù

### 和田玉 Hétián Yù — Hetian Nephrite

**Mineral:** nephrite (tremolite-actinolite), fibrous structure, exceptional toughness. Mohs hardness: 6.0–6.5.

**Region:** Xinjiang, Kunlun Mountains, Yurungkash River (白玉河 Báiyù Hé — "White Jade River") and Karakash River (墨玉河 Mòyù Hé — "Black Jade River").

**Colors:** the highest grade is 羊脂白玉 yángzhī báiyù, "mutton-fat white" — milky white with a slightly warm tone. Also green, yellow 黄玉 huángyù, black 墨玉 mòyù, and sugar-brown 糖玉 tángyù (with brown veins).

**Status:** the stone of Chinese civilization. As early as the Liangzhu culture (c. 3300–2300 BCE), it was used for ritual jades — the 琮 cóng and 璧 bì. Later, imperial seals of the Han dynasty. Today, hétián yù is the most expensive of the four: top material ranges from $1,000 to $10,000+ per gram. The same name 和田玉 is also applied to imported material — Russian 俄料 éliào and Korean 韩料 hánliào; these are cheaper.

### 独山玉 Dúshān Yù — Dushan Jade

**Mineral:** anorthitic rock — neither nephrite nor jadeite. Mohs hardness: 6.0–6.5.

**Region:** Dushan Hill near Nanyang city, Henan province.

**Colors:** polychrome — a very rare quality. Green, white, purple 独山紫 dúshān zǐ, yellow, red — often within a single piece. Green dúshān yù is easily mistaken for jadeite.

**Status:** mined since the Neolithic. Today, it is used for affordable carved objects, vases, and inexpensive jewelry. It occupies a mid-range position in the market.

### 岫玉 Xiù Yù — Xiuyan Jade

**Mineral:** serpentine. Mohs hardness: 2.5–5.5 (the softest of the four). Translucent, with a greasy luster.

**Region:** Xiuyan County, Liaoning province.

**Colors:** green tones, from pale chartreuse to deep emerald.

**Status:** the oldest of the Chinese jades in artifacts — it was used to carve "pig-dragon" figures 猪龙 zhūlóng of the Hongshan culture (c. 4700–2900 BCE). Today, it is the cheapest stone of the four, priced below $1 per gram. Its softness makes it suitable for large carvings and costume jewelry.

### 蓝田玉 Lántián Yù — Lantian Jade (Not Blue!)

**Mineral:** marble with silicates. Mohs hardness: 3–4. Color is pale, yellowish-green; there is no blue in it — the name is poetic.

**Region:** Lantian County, Shaanxi province.

**Status:** famous for the literary line by Li Shangyin in the poem "Jǐn Sè" (c. 858): 蓝田日暖玉生烟 — "Warm sun over Lantian, and jade gives birth to mist." Historical quarries were exhausted by the Song dynasty. Today, it is mostly used for souvenirs, figurines, and tiles. In some museum-trade versions of the list, lántián yù is replaced by turquoise — more on this below.

## Burmese Jadeite (缅甸翡翠 Miǎndiàn Fěicuì) — King of Jades

**Mineral:** jadeite (pyroxene). Mohs hardness: 6.5–7, glassy luster, higher toughness and strength than nephrite.

**Region:** Hpakant mine, Kachin State, Myanmar. This source yields ~95% of the world's jadeite.

**History in China:** First written mention in 1387 (Ming dynasty). A rare stone, with limited import. Large-scale inflow began only at the end of Emperor Qianlong's reign (regular tribute starting in 1784). Explosion in popularity in the 19th century, partly due to Empress Dowager Cixi, who collected jadeite. The finest grade is called "imperial green" 帝王绿 dìwáng lǜ.

**Why is jadeite not included in the "four jades"?** Because "sì dà míng yù" is a list of stones mined within China's territory. Jadeite is imported, from Myanmar. Thus, it is omitted from this list. But on the market, it is the "king of jades": vivid colors (especially green, which nephrite rarely achieves), Qing court patronage, hardness and luster, and a well-established supply chain.

## Turquoise — The Contested Fourth

In some modern reference works, turquoise 绿松石 lǜsōngshí replaces lántián yù. Supporters argue that turquoise has been used continuously in China since the Shang dynasty bronze inlays (c. 1200 BCE). Major Chinese deposits are at Zhushan 竹山 and Yunxian 郧县 in Hubei province (70–80% of China's production). However, there is no official decision, and most books still present the list with lántián yù.

## Stone on the Tea Table

Stone is part of tea culture, and not only in jewelry. Here is what you can actually encounter:

- **Stone tea trays.** Serpentine xiù yù and anorthitic dúshān yù are most common — they are cheap, easy to carve, and water-resistant. A tray of hétián yù or jadeite is rare due to price. A jadeite tray is displayed as a showpiece.
- **Jade cups.** Made from hétián yù or dúshān yù — smooth stone, pleasant to the touch. It stays slightly cool, so tea looks distinctive: the liquor's color appears brighter against it. Hardness (6+) protects the cup from scratches.
- **Desk figurines 文玩 wénwán.** The tradition of "scholar's objects" in stone — bat 蝙蝠 (symbol of happiness), fish 鱼 (prosperity), gourd 葫芦 (abundance). These carved pieces have moved from the study to the tea table, where they sit beside the teapot.

Stone does not react with tea or absorb aromas — a clean, practical material for vessels, if you are comfortable with the price.

## TL;DR

"The Four Famous Jades of China" is a modern list (post-1949): hétián yù (nephrite from Xinjiang), dúshān yù (anorthite from Henan), xiù yù (soft serpentine from Liaoning), lántián yù (marble from Shaanxi). Burmese jadeite 翡翠 fěicuì is not part of the list but is considered the "king of jades." On the tea table, stone appears in trays, cups, and desk figurines.

## FAQ

**Why is "sì dà míng yù" not an ancient concept?**
The list appeared in Chinese textbooks and reference works after 1949, as part of the systematization of the PRC's mineral resources. No ancient treatise mentions this quartet as a canon.

**What is the difference between hétián yù and jadeite (fěicuì)?**
Hétián yù is nephrite (tremolite-actinolite), tough with a fibrous structure and matte-silky luster. Fěicuì is jadeite (pyroxene), harder, with a glassy luster and more vivid green. They are two different minerals.

**Does turquoise belong to the "four jades"?**
No, it is not in the official list. In popular publications, turquoise (lǜsōngshí) sometimes substitutes for lántián yù because of its ancient use (Shang bronze inlays), but this is not a universal practice.

**Can you drink tea from a hétián yù cup?**
Yes. Hétián yù is chemically inert, does not absorb aromas, and withstands hot water. However, the cup is expensive and brittle if dropped (like any stone).

**Which stones are suitable for a tea tray?**
Serpentine xiù yù is cheap, soft, and easy to work. Anorthitic dúshān yù is harder, polychrome, and visually attractive. Jadeite and hétián yù are rare, expensive showpieces. Lántián yù (marble) is sensitive to acids (e.g., lemon in tea) and scratches.
