# Famous precious woods of China (名贵木材): huanghuali, chenxiang, Taihang cliff cypress — and why there is no fixed 'four'

## Introduction

In Chinese culture there is the concept of "four famous" (四大, sì dà), applied to various things: from classical novels to [jades](/wiki/four-famous-jades), [potteries](/wiki/four-famous-potteries), and, of course, precious woods. However, when it comes to "the four famous woods" (四大名木, sì dà míng mù), it must be honestly stated: **this is not an ancient imperial canon or a classification from Ming-era treatises**. It is a modern, flexible marketing label born in the wenwan (文玩, wénwán) market in the late 20th — early 21st century.

Within our series, which includes articles on [the four famous jades](/wiki/four-famous-jades) and [the four famous potteries](/wiki/four-famous-potteries), we again follow the rule: show historical truth, not promotional fiction.

In this text we will examine three main "versions" of the list, and then discuss in detail each of the five woods that most often appear in these lists. You will see that under one umbrella are gathered completely different materials: classical furniture timber, incense resin, and cliff cypress — a pure collecting phenomenon of the 2010s. What unites them today is the 手串 (shǒuchuàn) bracelet.

## Classical vs. modern list

To understand how this confusion arose, we must return to the Ming and Qing eras. The classical tradition of "hardwood" (硬木, yìngmù) for furniture had a clear hierarchy. For the imperial court and highest aristocracy there was a "big three": huanghuali (黄花梨), zitan (紫檀), and jinsi nan (金丝楠). These were the three main materials for furniture, architecture, and carving.

Among cabinetmakers, there was a saying **一黄二紫三红** (yī huáng, èr zǐ, sān hóng): first — huanghuali (黄, huáng), second — zitan (紫, zǐ), third — "old red wood" (老红木, lǎo hóngmù) — a type of rosewood. This is the classical, documentarily confirmed ranking.

There was no "four" in the classics. The modern wenwan market has produced at least three competing versions of "四大名木":

1. **Luxury version**: Hainan huanghuali (海南黄花梨) · xiaoye zitan (小叶紫檀) · jinsi nan (金丝楠) · chenxiang (沉香, agarwood).
2. **Incense and wenwan version**: Hainan huanghuali · xiaoye zitan · qinan (奇楠, top-grade chenxiang) · laoshan tanxiang (老山檀香, sandalwood).
3. **Cypress version**: Hainan huanghuali · xiaoye zitan · jinsi nan · Taihang cliff cypress (太行崖柏).

None of these foursomes is "canonical". The choice depends on the seller and target audience. We will examine all five main materials, each deserving separate attention.

## Hainan huanghuali (海南黄花梨)

*Species: Dalbergia odorifera. Endemic to Hainan Island.*

Huanghuali (黄花梨, huánghuālí) is the "king of Ming furniture" (明式家具之王, míngshì jiājù zhī wáng). In the classical hardwood hierarchy it stands first: "一黄". This wood is valued for its unique color play: from golden through orange to wine-red over years. The grain can form characteristic "ghost faces" (鬼脸, guǐliǎn) — concentric whorls resembling masks. Its fragrance is noble, sweet-spicy, with notes of rosewood — the so-called "jiangxiang aroma" (降香).

Huanghuali is so hard and dense that in classical furniture it was almost never lacquered: wax polishing was enough to reveal the grain. This is the ideal of Ming-style aesthetics: clean lines, minimal ornament, maximum natural beauty.

*Status today:* Dalbergia odorifera is listed in CITES Appendix II (the entire genus Dalbergia since 2023 after CoP19). In China it is a Category II protected plant. Wild logging is prohibited. All legal wood is either pre-1949 stock (老料, lǎo liào), material from dismantling old houses and ships, or permitted cultivated material (新料, xīn liào) — paler in color and weaker in fragrance.

*Nuance:* On the market, Vietnamese huanghuali (Dalbergia tonkinensis) is often passed off as Hainan. They are different species: Vietnamese is noticeably lighter, with less expressive grain and almost no aroma. More details in the article [huanghuali](/wiki/huanghuali).

*Price:* The most expensive wood in the world by volume. Top figured antique material — $50,000–200,000+/kg. A quality bracelet — $100–1000.

## Xiaoye zitan (小叶紫檀)

*Species: Pterocarpus santalinus, India.*

Zitan (紫檀, zǐtán) is the "king of Qing furniture". In the classical hierarchy it holds second place. Its distinguishing feature is extreme density. It is the heaviest of common furniture woods in China. Freshly cut, the wood is orange-red, but under light and air it oxidizes to a deep purplish-black with a mirror-like patina. Characteristic are golden mineral inclusions — "golden stars" (金星, jīnxīng).

The saying "千年紫檀，百年黄花梨" (zitan — a thousand years, huanghuali — a hundred years) describes not so much exact age as relative growth rate: zitan grows extremely slowly, so its reserves were depleted first.

*Status:* Pterocarpus santalinus has been listed in CITES Appendix II since 1975. Export of wild wood is zero. In legal trade, only plantation-grown material.

*Note:* "Xiaoye" (小葉, xiǎoyè) means "small-leaf" — this is not a botanical subspecies but a trade name referring to leaf shape. "Large-leaf zitan" (大葉紫檀, dàyè zǐtán) is a completely different species (Pterocarpus macrocarpus), much inferior in density and color. More details in the article [xiaoye zitan](/wiki/xiao-ye-zitan).

## Jinsi nan (金丝楠)

*Species: Phoebe zhennan (桢楠) and Phoebe bournei (闽楠). Family Lauraceae.*

Jinsi nan (金丝楠, jīnsī nán) is "golden-thread nanmu". It is a broadleaf angiosperm, not a conifer, yet **surprisingly light and soft** for a precious wood. Its main virtue is an optical effect: the cut reveals shimmering golden threads resembling water ripples (水波纹, shuǐbōwén) or clouds (云彩纹, yúncǎiwén). The fragrance is sweet, medicinal, and repels insects.

In the Qing era, jinsi nan was the main structural material for imperial palaces. The most famous columns of the Forbidden City (e.g., in the Hall of Supreme Harmony) are made from it. The saying goes: "Golden-thread nanmu does not rot for a thousand years" (金丝楠千年不腐, jīnsī nán qiānnián bù fǔ).

*Status:* Both species (Phoebe zhennan and P. bournei) are Category II protected plants in China. Wild logging is prohibited. Only wood from dismantling old structures (老料, lǎo liào) or plantation material is available.

*Price:* Old material — $200–500/kg. Top figured — $2,000–10,000+/kg.

## Chenxiang (沉香)

*Resin from trees of the genus Aquilaria.*

Chenxiang (沉香, chénxiāng) is **not wood**. It is an aromatic resin produced by a sick or fungus-infected Aquilaria tree in response to injury. Healthy, light-colored Aquilaria wood has no smell and no value. The more resin accumulated in the fibers, the heavier and more aromatic the material. The best pieces sink in water — hence the term "沉水" (chénshuǐ, "sinking"). The highest grade is qinan (奇楠, qínán): a soft, gel-like resin with a changing aroma, the most expensive natural material in the world by weight.

Chenxiang traditionally belongs not to furniture culture but to **incense** (香, xiāng) and medicinal culture. It is called the "king of incense" (香中之王, xiāng zhōng zhī wáng) and is used in ceremonies, meditation, and traditional Chinese medicine. It entered the modern list of "famous woods" as a collectible material for bracelets and miniature carvings.

*Contrast with tanxiang:* Sandalwood (檀香, tánxiāng) is *Santalum album*, ordinary wood that is itself aromatic (in the heartwood). It is used for carving and basic incense sticks. Chenxiang, on the other hand, is a pathological resin that can only be polished (or distilled for terpenes). More details: [chenxiang](/wiki/chenxiang) and [tanxiang](/wiki/tanxiang).

*Status:* Wild Aquilaria are protected species (CITES App. II for some species, Chinese national laws). In legal trade, only plantation induction (artificial infection) is available.

*Price:* Common wild incense — $10–50/g. Sinking grade (沉水, chénshuǐ) — $200–1000/g. Qinan — $5,000–20,000+/g. Plantation, induced (for bracelets) — $1–5/g.

*Warning:* This is the most counterfeited material in the world. 95% of bracelets on the market are oil-soaked, fragrance-added plantation wood or synthetics.

## Taihang cliff cypress (太行崖柏)

*Trade name: Taihang yabai (太行崖柏, tàiháng yábǎi). Reality:*

This is a pure 2010s phenomenon. Before this, the term "崖柏" (yábǎi) was known only to dendrologists as the scientific name for *Thuja sutchuenensis* — an extremely rare cypress from Sichuan/Chongqing, nearly extinct in the wild. However, in 2010–2014, a mass of material under this name flooded the wenwan market from the Taihang Mountains (Shanxi/Hebei). Botanical analysis showed: this is **not Thuja sutchuenensis**, but common oriental arborvitae (侧柏, *Platycladus orientalis*) growing on cliffs. The entire boom was based on confusion or deliberate substitution.

*Cause of the boom:* wild stories about "thousand-year-old cliff trees", false health myths, and online PR. Price peak — 2014, then collapse by 2018. Today it is a niche wenwan material.

*Attractive qualities:* intense, sharp menthol-camphor sweet aroma (stronger than huanghuali). Aged, weathered wood (陈化料, chénhuà liào) is most valued: it is dry, with cracks and a patina of time. The grain can show "sparrow eyes" (雀眼, quèyǎn) — small dark dots.

*Use:* exclusively bracelets (手串) and small carvings. Furniture is never made from it.

*Price today (post-boom):* common bracelets — $20–50; good weathered wood — $50–150; top — $200–500. Far from the 2014 peak.

More details: [Taihang cliff cypress](/wiki/taihang-cliff-cypress).

## What unites them today: bracelet culture 手串

In the 2000s–2010s, a mass wenwan boom (文玩热, wénwán rè) occurred in China. Buddhist prayer beads (念珠, niànzhū), feng shui, and status consumption merged into one — **bracelet culture 手串 (shǒuchuàn)**. Each wood received its "purpose":

- **Huanghuali**: for connoisseurs of grain and aroma. Worn as a sign of taste.
- **Zitan**: for feeling the weight and luster. "Golden stars" signify status.
- **Jinsi nan**: for soft shimmer and calmness.
- **Chenxiang**: for meditation, subtle aroma. Very small beads, sky-high prices — or a fake.
- **Yabai (cypress)**: for a strong, "masculine" smell. Affordable price (post-boom).

As a result, furniture timber, incense resin, and cliff shrub ended up on the same shelf in the shop. This is precisely why "the four famous" is a purely market-driven list, not a historical one.

## Fakes

**Huanghuali.** Vietnamese *D. tonkinensis* is passed off as Hainan, as well as dyed rosewood or glued sawdust. It can be identified by smell (an alcohol test — real gives a spicy aroma) and under UV light (real gives a purple fluorescence; see [huanghuali](/wiki/huanghuali)).

**Zitan.** Large-leaf *P. macrocarpus* (cheap substitute), dyed hornbeam, or bloodwood. Best test — alcohol/water: real gives a blood-red extract, fakes give a weak one.

**Nanmu.** Ordinary *Phoebe* dyed and rolled with "gold", or plastic with glitter. Real jinsi nan gives a *living* shimmer when tilted in light, not a uniform sparkle.

**Chenxiang.** Over 95% is fake: plantation *Aquilaria* wood soaked in oils (usually to imitate the sinking fraction). Or epoxy resin with fragrance. Test: heat a needle — real gives a complex, multi-layered scent; fakes give a simple synthetic tone.

**Cypress (yabai).** Instead of cliff wood, ordinary plains *Platycladus orientalis* soaked in oil is sold. Signs: too uniform, overly sweet aroma, few cracks, light weight (cliff wood is denser). Tea boiling and chemicals imitate the weathered look.

## TL;DR

The expression "four famous woods of China" is a modern market construct, not a classical canon. This list equally includes: classical furniture timber (huanghuali, zitan), architectural wood (jinsi nan), incense resin (chenxiang), and a 2010s phenomenon (Taihang cypress). All are today united by hand bracelet culture 手串. The true "four" is defined by the seller: there are at least three competing versions. Be cautious: chenxiang is the most counterfeited material in the world, and "Taihang cliff cypress" turns out to be 99% plains arborvitae.

## FAQ

**What is 四大名木 (sì dà míng mù)?**
It is "the four famous woods" — a marketing term from the wenwan market, not an ancient canon. Different versions hide from 4 to 6 different woods.

**Why is there no single list of "the four"?**
Classical sources know only a "big three" for furniture (huanghuali, zitan, red wood). Chenxiang (a resin, not wood) and cliff cypress (a pure 2010s phenomenon) were introduced into the modern list, preventing a canon from forming.

**Which huanghuali is more expensive — Hainan or Vietnamese?**
Hainan (D. odorifera) is dozens of times more expensive. Vietnamese (D. tonkinensis) is paler, without aroma, and has an unexpressive grain. On the market it is often sold as Hainan.

**What is the difference between chenxiang and tanxiang?**
Chenxiang is the resin of a diseased Aquilaria tree, used in incense and medicine. Tanxiang is the ordinary aromatic heartwood of sandalwood (S. album), used for carving and basic incense.

**How common are fakes of chenxiang?**
Extremely common. Estimates suggest over 95% of bracelets on the market are oil-soaked plantation wood or synthetics with fragrance. Real sinking-grade chenxiang is an exceptional rarity.

**Is Taihang cliff cypress Thuja sutchuenensis?**
In the vast majority of cases — no. Market material from Taihang is Platycladus orientalis (arborvitae) growing on cliffs. True Thuja sutchuenensis (崖柏 in a dendrological sense) is nearly extinct and does not appear in trade.

**What are "golden stars" in zitan?**
They are natural mineral inclusions (calcite, quartz) in the wood vessels. They appear as shiny dots under light. "Golden stars" increase the price, but they are easily faked by spraying brass dust (which gives an unnaturally bright, structureless shine).
