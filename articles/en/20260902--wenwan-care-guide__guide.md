# Pánwán (盘玩) — how to work wenwan: wood, seeds, stone

Some things you buy finished. Wenwan is not one of them. A strand arrives as a blank — dull, matte, nobody's yet. From there you work it by hand, for months, and only then do you get the thing you were after: **patina, 包浆 (bāojiāng)**, a dense warm film that makes the surface look lit from inside.

The process is called **pánwán (盘玩)** — literally "to roll and play." Simple word, awkward practice: some materials demand bare hands, others are ruined permanently by them, and the two are easier to confuse than you'd think.

This guide is assembled from Chinese sources — collector forums, dealers, specialist sites like 崖柏之家. We have tried to keep what is evidenced separate from what is passed along as lore. There is a difference, and it matters.

**TL;DR:** it comes down to two questions. Does the material supply its own oil, or does the oil come off your hands? And do you want it to darken? Answer both and the method follows.

## What this covers

**Wenwan (文玩, wénwán)** in its modern sense means objects worn on the body or held in the hand and deliberately worked with over time. The term used to be broader — the scholar's playthings: brush pots, inkstones, seals, the 文房四宝 of the writing desk. Today, when someone says 文玩 they almost certainly mean 文玩手串 (wénwán shǒuchuàn): strands, single beads, something carried in a pocket and 盘'd.

**Not covered:** furniture, [teaware](/wiki/chinese-teaware), [Yixing pots](/wiki/yixing-zisha), [censers](/wiki/xianglu). Their surfaces change too, but through use rather than deliberate handling. A teapot's patina comes from tea; a table's from wax and years. Nothing below transfers to them.

### The three categories

The Chinese market divides wenwan into three families by origin:

1. **Seeds, 籽类** — bodhi seeds, [walnuts](/wiki/wenwan-walnuts), olive pits, gourds
2. **Wood, 木类** — [yabai](/wiki/taihang-cliff-cypress), [huanghuali](/wiki/huanghuali), [zitan](/wiki/xiao-ye-zitan), [agarwood](/wiki/chenxiang)
3. **Stone, 玉石类** — [nephrite](/wiki/four-famous-jades), jadeite, agate, turquoise, **and amber and beeswax amber (琥珀蜜蜡)**

> **About amber.** It genuinely does sit on the "stone" shelf — that is how the trade is organised, and there is no point arguing with it. But behaviourally it is not a stone at all. Mohs 2–3, barely harder than a fingernail; it builds a real sebum film that has to cure; and it degrades permanently from mistakes nephrite simply shrugs off. Handling it like stone is the fastest way to ruin it. Keep it in the stone category, but give it its own protocol. Details in the Amber section below.

The origin-based split is worth knowing if only because every dealer uses it. **But it does not predict behaviour.** The real line falls elsewhere — on where the oil comes from:

- **Seeds, stone and amber** have no usable oil of their own. You supply it.
- **Wood** mostly supplies its own, and your skin oil is a contaminant.

So a walnut and a jade bead want much the same thing — bare hands, sustained contact — while a walnut and a yabai strand want opposite treatments, though both are organic and both get "played."

Within wood there is one more line, and oil content alone does not settle it: **do you want the piece to darken?** That question separates zitan from huanghuali, though both are dense oily hardwoods.

And separately: **nephrite is the one material you cannot get wrong by handling.** No over-playing, no blackening, no exhausting it, no rest schedule to keep. Wear it or don't. In a document otherwise about things that punish inattention, it is the outlier.

## The governing principle

### Where the oil comes from

Two families, two genuinely different processes that happen to look alike.

**Self-supplying materials** — 崖柏 (yabai), 黄花梨 (huanghuali), 小叶紫檀 (zitan), 沉香 (chenxiang). These woods hold resins and volatile oils. Friction makes heat, heat drives the internal oil toward the surface, and there it oxidises into a hard film. Your skin oil contributes almost nothing useful and mostly contributes dirt.

The evidence here is clean: **low-oil yabai barely patinas at all.** White sapwood and damp material will not develop 包浆 however much you work them — and the same sources note that the oil is also the basis of the scent: no oil, no fragrance. If sebum were the feedstock, poor material would patina as readily as good material. It does not.

**Externally-supplied materials** — 文玩核桃 (walnut), 菩提子 (bodhi seeds), 文玩葫芦 (gourds), 和田玉 (nephrite), 蜜蜡 (amber). There is essentially nothing extractable inside: the walnut shell is lignified endocarp, the gourd a dried pericarp, jade a mineral. Here the patina is built from **your** sweat and sebum, deposited into surface micro-topography and oxidised in place.

Chinese sources say it plainly for jade: sweat and sebum gradually coating the surface evenly, oxidised by air into a film whose brightness produces attractive diffuse reflection. For amber the description is more specific still — a waxy crystalline substance precipitated out of a sebum mixture.

### Why rest matters

Any material that builds a real film needs drying time between sessions, because the film has to harden **before** more feedstock lands on top. That covers all the oily woods, the seeds, gourds and amber.

**Nephrite is the exception.** Its change is thin and largely optical: oil filling micro-topography, raising apparent gloss and translucency. There is no thick film to cure, so there is no rest phase. That is part of why it is so forgiving.

### The second variable: darken or not

Oil content alone does not decide the glove question. Darkening does.

- **Zitan** — high oil, and darkening is the entire point. Bare hands, no problem.
- **Huanghuali** — high oil, but darkening buries the figure you paid for. Gloves.
- **Yabai** — high oil, darkening is bad, and handling also costs you scent. Gloves, and minimally.
- **Walnuts and most seeds** — no oil of their own, and reddening is desirable. Bare hands are mandatory.
- **Chenxiang** — resin-rich, but a patina seals the pores the fragrance escapes through. Do not play it at all.

### The shared mechanism

In both families the sequence is identical:

> **Friction delivers oil to the surface. Oxidation during rest turns it into a film.**

Rubbing does not create patina. It only supplies feedstock. Air does the work. This is why every tradition alternates handling with resting — **七盘七放** (qī pán qī fàng, "seven times played, seven times set down"), **少盘多放** (shǎo pán duō fàng, "play less, rest more"). Continuous rubbing never lets the oxidation step finish.

## Tools

There are three, and they are not interchangeable. Each does one job.

### Cotton gloves

They burnish a smooth surface, transmitting friction and heat without transferring sweat or sebum. They only work where they can reach — that is, on smooth material.

- **Thin, undyed, plain white.** The cheap jewellery or parade type. No dyed gloves — dye transfers onto light material. No PVC grip dots. No terry loops that snag cord.
- **Rinse new gloves in plain water and dry before first use**, to remove loose lint and sizing.
- **One glove set per item.** Yabai resin will scent a glove; that glove has no business near chenxiang afterwards.

**Cotton or microfibre.** The Chinese split is by hand type, not by material: dry hands can use cotton, heavy-sweat hands are steered toward non-breathable faux suede, because cotton soaks through. For yabai, prefer cotton regardless — microfibre is engineered to absorb oil aggressively, and the whole reason for short gloved sessions is to stop the glove wicking away the resin you are trying to surface.

**Washing.** Hand wash, mild unscented soap, rinse very thoroughly, air dry flat.

- **Never fabric softener or dryer sheets.** They deposit cationic surfactants and silicone which transfer straight onto the wood as a film. This is the single worst thing you can do to a play glove, and with microfibre it clogs the fibre permanently.
- **No bleach or brightener-heavy detergent.**
- **Wash when gritty or dirty, not on a schedule.** A glove with embedded dust is sandpaper. But a lightly resin-seasoned glove works better than a fresh one, so don't over-wash. The rule of thumb: don't keep using a glove that has gone black.
- **Replace** when thinning or pilling.

### Soft brush

Clears grime out of texture and pores. Reaches where a glove physically cannot.

The brush is **not** a patina tool for smooth wood. In Chinese guides for yabai and huanghuali it appears only for dust removal. Its real home is textured material: 金刚菩提 and [walnuts](/wiki/wenwan-walnuts), where crevices trap grime and a glove cannot enter at all. It is also standard and ongoing for jade.

### Cloth and bags

- **Dry cotton cloth** — wiping after wear.
- **Barely damp cotton cloth** — the only permitted cleaning for wood that is genuinely dirty. Then let it sit before resuming.
- **Sealed plastic bag** — for yabai, to force oil to the surface (闷油, mèn yóu). Also for chenxiang storage.
- **Pure cotton bag, towel-lined** — for huanghuali. Soft, breathable, protects from knocks while still letting air reach the beads.

The bag choice is not cosmetic. Sealed and breathable do opposite jobs.

## Taihang yabai 崖柏

**Prized for:** scent first, then the glassy 玻璃底 finish.
**Main risk:** losing the fragrance to over-handling.

### The frequency question

Sources genuinely disagree, and it is more useful to know both camps than to treat one as consensus.

**Mainstream method.** Glove-play 20–30 minutes, then seal in the bag for two days to 闷油, remove, play again. Both hands, whole strand rolled between the palms — explicitly **not** one-handed on one section, which produces uneven patina. Another source gives it more simply: daily 30 minutes then back in the bag, heat drawing the oil out, patina in about a month. This is described as the traditional method for ordinary black-oil and red-oil beads.

**Low-frequency method.** The same site offers this as an explicit alternative: play one day for 15–30 minutes, then leave it two to seven days, and over roughly 60 days it will patina anyway. One dealer puts it as 多放少盘 — rest more, play less: a week resting, one gloved session, or even resting with no playing at all. 崖柏之家 reduces the whole method to two characters: 少盘.

**Both work.** The low-frequency method costs you less scent. That is the reason to prefer it, not that daily is wrong.

Session length runs from 3–5 minutes (stop as soon as the beads brighten) at the conservative end to 20–30 minutes at the mainstream end.

### The sequence

**Phase 0 — acclimatise, 2–3 weeks.** High-oil yabai should be sealed on arrival so it adjusts to the local climate without cracking.

**Phase 1 — gloved sessions, ~2 weeks to 1 month.** Cotton gloves, both hands, whole strand between the palms. Cover every area, especially around the bead holes. Seal between sessions. Store cool and shaded.

**The milestone.** After one to two weeks you feel a slight drag or tackiness when handling — that is the thin patina. Important: this is a signal to **stop and rest**, not to push on. Rubbing during the sticky phase interferes with oxidation and actually slows patina formation. Wait until the tackiness is gone, then resume.

**The end of sealing.** At about one month the surface goes very bright — 半包浆, half-patina. Stop sealing; leave the bag open so it gets air, for another 15 to 30 days. The reason for sealing during roughly the first 20 days is that the oil needs to emerge fully so it can be spread evenly. Exposing it to air too soon makes the oil harden unevenly and produces patchy patina thickness.

**Phase 2 — 醒珠, "waking the beads."** Once a patina has formed, wear it for about a week to let it oxidise and harden naturally. **Do not rub during this period.** Wearing is a step in the cycle, not an alternative to it.

**Phase 3 — repeat.** Cycle rubbing and wearing five or six times. At two to three months the reflective 玻璃底 quality appears. Between three and six months it is stable and can be worn and handled normally.

### Bare hands

Only after the patina exists — roughly a month of glove cycles. The sequence in 崖柏之家 runs: let it exude oil, let the surface oxidise into a hard bright layer, then light cotton-glove work, and only then bare hands.

Going bare too early produces 盘黑 or 盘花 — sweat-driven blackening and blotchy uneven colour. Not reversible.

There is a specific failure mode, 黏糊糊: high-oil yabai plus bare hands turns tacky and will not rub smooth. The fix is to go back to cotton gloves and work gently — an hour on, then a day of rest.

### Rules

- Never significant water. Barely damp cotton cloth if dirty, then let it sit.
- Do not rub hard or fast: the oil surfaces before it can oxidise and the glove absorbs it, which can ruin the piece.
- Do not rub for long stretches: losing too much oil damages both the patina and the fragrance.
- Not in real heat. Yabai without a thick patina is unsuited to long summer wear — wear two days, then rest and shade-dry two days.
- Off for hand-washing, toilet, showers.
- Do not oil it.

### The 闻香 option

Chinese sellers treat 闻香手串 — scent bracelets — as a separate category needing no patina at all: kept in a storage jar, picked up to smell. If the fragrance is what you actually value in a piece, this is a legitimate choice rather than a failure to follow through.

### White frost 白霜

Frost forming on sealed yabai is real and has a mechanism. It is largely cedrol, a crystalline solid at room temperature with enough vapour pressure to sublimate and recrystallise on the surface; sealing saturates the local air and the crystals deposit. It correlates with oil content.

Two cautions. It is temperature-driven — cool storage produces it faster, warm storage suppresses it — so absence of frost in a hot month tells you little. And it is fakeable by dusting with cedrol or borneol powder. **Real frost regrows after you wipe it off. Fake frost does not.**

**One thing sealing does not do:** regenerate lost scent. When you open a bag and get a strong hit, most of that is accumulated headspace, not the wood having recovered. Volatiles that left during handling do not come back. Resting genuinely helps, in that deeper oil migrates outward over time, but the dramatic "waking of the aroma" is mostly the bag, and it fades within minutes.

## Huanghuali 黄花梨

**Prized for:** the figure, 花纹.
**Main risk:** blackening that buries the grain.

Huanghuali is **not** sealed. Where yabai gets bagged to force oil out, huanghuali rests in open air, because oxygen contact is what forms the film. Getting this backwards is the main way people go wrong switching between the two.

### The governing risk

Bare-hand play turns huanghuali blackish-brown and buries the figure, which is why it needs more patience than zitan — with zitan the darkening **is** the point. Anyone with damp or sweaty hands is advised to play less, use gloves, and let natural oxidation do most of the work.

### The sequence

**Phase 0 — acclimatise, 3–4 days.** Open the sealed bag a small slit and leave it in a box so it adjusts to the local climate. An alternative: put the beads in a towel bag pulled inside out for 7–10 days, which protects them and lightly re-polishes the surface at the same time.

**Phase 1 — remove the factory wax.** Pure cotton gloves, several passes, clearing dust and any wax applied for appearance. 10–20 minutes a day, with attention to the gaps between beads. A more aggressive version uses a rough 搓澡巾 towel for two or three days; not recommended on beads whose surface you care about.

**Phase 2 — 挂放, hang it, about two weeks.** This step has no yabai equivalent. Hang the cleaned strand in open air and light for roughly half a month, letting the oil slowly emerge and cure and letting the wood stabilise in your environment.

**Phase 3 — 少盘多放.** Gloved sessions, from a few minutes to half an hour, then rest. Hands dry before every session. Between sessions store it in a pure cotton bag, ideally towel-lined — soft and breathable, protecting from knocks while letting air reach the beads.

**Phase 4 — bare hands.** Only after the glove-and-rest cycles. Hands just washed and completely dry, no lotion or sunscreen residue. Cover every part of each bead including around the holes. About half an hour a day.

**The milestone.** After one to two weeks of bare-hand work you feel a slight drag — the thin patina. Rest about a week to let it harden. Then repeat the play-and-rest pairing.

Around three months the luster comes up. A stable patina takes roughly half a year, at which point it still should not meet water, oil, or dirt.

### Rules

- Never water. Barely damp cotton cloth if dirty. If it does get wet, dry immediately and then work it with cotton gloves for a few days — never in sun, never a hairdryer.
- Off for hand-washing and showers.
- Away from perfume, alcohol, cleaners, strong light, damp.
- Away from knocks against hard objects.
- Do not oil it.

Huanghuali is 降香黄檀 and genuinely fragrant, so the handling-versus-scent tradeoff applies here too, just less acutely than with yabai.

## Zitan 小叶紫檀

**Prized for:** deep wine-purple colour and density.
**Main risk:** there is barely one — and that is the point.

[Zitan](/wiki/xiao-ye-zitan) is the most forgiving of the oily woods, because the one thing that could go wrong is the thing you want. Darkening is the goal. Fresh zitan is orange-red; over months it moves to dark wine, nearly black in reflected light, and that is a sign of work done right rather than a piece spoiled.

Hence bare hands, with no gloved preliminary phase. Many people still start with a few days of gloves to strip the factory wax and even out the surface, but that is hygiene, not protection.

What survives from the general rules: light rest between sessions (the film has to oxidise here as anywhere), dry hands, no water, no knocks. Zitan is denser than water — a bead sinks — but it cracks readily from sharp humidity swings, so dry winter heating is as unkind to it as to the rest.

Stable patina: three to six months.

## Chenxiang 沉香

**Prized for:** scent, entirely.
**Main risk:** sealing the pores the scent escapes through.

[Agarwood](/wiki/chenxiang) is generally **not suited to 盘玩 at all.** A patina layer can block the surface pores so the fragrance cannot escape, weakening or eliminating the scent. Only rare high-oil pieces keep their fragrance while forming one.

**Protocol:**

- Wear intermittently. Continuous wear burns through the oils faster.
- Wipe with a **slightly damp** cotton cloth, then shade-dry. The point is lifting accumulated grime.
- Sealed storage when not worn.
- No rubbing, no brushing, no gloved sessions.
- Off in the shower — shampoo, body wash and detergent corrode agarwood and thin its scent.
- Do not oil it.

**On the common claim that agarwood's scent is "resin, not volatile oil":** this is wrong. Oud oil is steam-distilled, which only works on volatiles. The real difference from yabai is molecular weight — agarwood is dominated by heavy sesquiterpenes and chromones that evaporate slowly, while yabai leans on lighter monoterpenes plus cedrol that move fast. It is a difference of rate, not of category. Practically: yabai loses scent faster than agarwood for the same handling.

## Seeds

No internal oil. Sebum is the entire feedstock, so **bare hands are mandatory** — gloves would defeat the purpose.

They are not equally forgiving:

| Seed | Notes |
|---|---|
| **凤眼菩提** Phoenix-eye | Dense, tolerant, reddens well. Best of the seeds for daily counting. |
| **星月菩提** Moon-and-stars | Bare hands, but sweat causes permanent blotching. Cloth or glove work is standard first, to lay down a base polish before bare hands. |
| **金刚菩提** Vajra | Where the boar brush genuinely belongs. Crevices trap grime and go black in the ridges without it. |
| **文玩核桃** [Walnut](/wiki/wenwan-walnuts) | Bare hands plus brushing, the classic combination. New walnuts should get a brush-only phase first, to clear the grooves and let colour come up evenly. |
| **橄榄核** Olive pit | Cracks with humidity swings. A real risk in heated winter interiors. |

### Where gloves do fit on seeds

Gloves and brushes are not substitutes:

- **Glove** burnishes a smooth surface. Needs smoothness to reach it.
- **Brush** clears texture and pores. Reaches where a glove cannot.

On 星月 and 凤眼, mostly smooth, gloves work well as a preparatory and protective tool. On 金刚 and walnuts a glove cannot enter the ridges at all — the brush is the only tool that touches the parts that matter.

Two refinements to the mechanism: rubbing spreads **oil**, not patina — patina forms in place by oxidation. And warming does less on a seed than on oily wood, since heat's main job on yabai is mobilising internal resin and a seed has none. It still helps work deposited sebum into the surface, but the effect is smaller.

**The structural difference:** on oil-rich wood, gloves can be the **primary** tool, because the wood supplies the material and the glove only has to work it. On seeds, gloves are a **finishing** tool, because they cannot supply what your hands supply. That is why seeds eventually require bare hands and yabai does not.

Each type is covered in detail in [Wenwan seeds](/wiki/wenwan-seeds).

### Gourds 文玩葫芦

Conventionally their own category, 匏器, but mechanically they belong here: a dried pericarp with no usable oil of its own, worked bare-handed toward a deep colour. 手捻葫芦 — the small palm-sized kind rolled in one hand — are the ones most often played.

**The two preparatory steps are what decide the outcome.**

*Curing.* A new gourd must be fully shade-dried before you touch it. Residual moisture inside a hollow shell means mould, and mould inside a gourd is not recoverable. Green gourds turn yellow as they cure, and this takes months. Do not seal a new gourd in plastic.

*Skinning.* Gourds carry a whitish waxy outer skin that has to come off before playing, or the colour comes up patchy underneath it. Traditionally removed with very fine abrasive or a scouring pad once the gourd is dry.

**Then it is a seed protocol.** Bare hands, sustained rolling, play-and-rest cycling. The progression runs pale yellow → orange → deep red, and on good material eventually a translucent 枣红 "date red" or agate-like tone. One to three years.

**Skin quality decides everything**, and this mirrors the low-oil-yabai point exactly. A 糠皮 gourd — spongy, underdeveloped skin — will never take a good colour no matter how long you work it. 老皮 with a dense hard surface is what patinas. As with wood, the material sets the ceiling and technique only determines whether you reach it.

**Risks:** mould from incomplete curing, cracking from humidity swings, denting because the shell is thin, and bleaching from sun. A soft brush for dust is fine, and necessary on textured types — 押花 or moulded 范制 gourds with relief patterns.

## Jade

[Jade](/wiki/four-famous-jades) sits on the walnut side of the principle: external supply, no internal oil.

Which means the 少盘多放 logic does not apply. Nothing inside to draw out, nothing to exhaust, no scent to spend. **No sealed bag, no rest phase.** Jade is the one material here where more handling is simply better.

### Two inversions from the wood

**Water is fine, and required.** New nephrite should be soaked in warm water — never hot — for about 20 minutes, wiped with a clean white cloth, drained, then brushed gently and evenly with a soft brush. This removes the industrial paraffin wax applied at the factory, which sits in the pores blocking the stone's real character. Another source gives a two-to-three-hour soak in clean water with a soft brush. Merchants wax jade to brighten it; warm-water washing and shade-drying before starting is standard.

**A brush is permitted, and useful on carved pieces.** Occasional light brushing clears dust that would otherwise settle into surface crevices and become hard to remove. The exact opposite of the yabai advice — but on plain round beads it is largely optional. Wearing and handling a smooth strand keeps it clean by itself.

**No gloves.** Bare skin is the supply.

### Method

**文盘** ("civil play") is what you want: clean hands, one to two hours of daily handling, body oils doing the work, initial patina visible in three to six months.

**武盘** ("martial play") is the version where beads are made to knock and rub against each other with the accompanying sound. Specifically not recommended — it damages the strand.

### Which jade

**Nephrite 和田玉** responds well. Colour can also even out over time, particularly in 青白玉 and 糖玉.

**Jadeite 翡翠** is much denser and glassier and responds far less. With jadeite the goal is preservation rather than transformation.

**Hard warning:** B货 jadeite — acid-bleached and resin-impregnated — must never be oiled or heavily handled. The resin yellows and crazes. Without a certificate, treat any jadeite piece as unknown.

### The real risk

Unlike wood, the failure mode is not discolouration but fracture. Jade is tough, but a sharp knock can start an internal flaw that surfaces months later. Remove it before anything physical. No ultrasonic cleaners, no boiling, no sudden temperature changes. Avoid perfume, alcohol, cleaners, bleach.

## Amber

**Prized for:** translucency, and the deepening red of age.
**Main risk:** everything. This is the least forgiving material in the hobby.

Amber is fossilised resin, filed under stone by market convention only. Mechanically it belongs nowhere else in this document. Mohs 2–3, only slightly harder than a fingernail.

### It patinas readily — and the film is yours

Amber is something of a marvel among wenwan materials for how quickly it takes a patina despite being an organic gem. The film is described specifically as a waxy crystalline substance precipitated out of a sebum mixture — so it sits with seeds and jade on the external-supply side.

Its physical state is unstable at first. As you continue playing, fresh sebum softens the layer already formed, and the surface feels sticky or oily-returning. That is normal, not a fault.

### The cycle

Smooth playing → drag appears → set aside to dry → smooth playing again, repeated. The drag shows after roughly ten days of slightly-damp-hand work and means a thin patina has formed; that is the point to stop and let it cure.

A coarser rhythm given elsewhere: play a day, then a pass; play a week, ease off two days; play a month, clean it once.

Same drag milestone as huanghuali and yabai, from an entirely different mechanism.

### 退油 — the failure unique to amber

Abandon the process halfway and blotches appear on the surface, and the material is meaningfully worse than before you started. Amber punishes half-finished work in a way wood does not. Either commit to the cycle or leave the piece alone.

### Handling constraints

- **Never a stiff brush or a blade.** It scratches instantly. Guides list this as a common and irreversible mistake.
- **Never ultrasonic.** The vibration can propagate existing internal fissures into full cracks.
- **Never sun or a heat source.** High temperature drives off moisture, leaving it dry and dull, and can soften the surface into tackiness.
- **Store away from harder stones.** Anything above Mohs 3 will scratch it in a drawer.
- **Alcohol, perfume and acetone dissolve amber.** This is the rule the Chinese guides assume rather than state.
- **Wear it.** Sources are pointed about this: hoarding an amber strand unworn is treated as a care mistake, since it becomes more lustrous and translucent through contact.

### Cleaning

At about the one-month mark it will look dirty. The recommendation is a soft toothbrush with colourless baby oil, working the crevices, then blotted dry with a towel — **no water**. Some guides also suggest topping up the oil when the surface feels dry and draggy.

Be cautious with added oil. It is folk practice rather than anything established, and it attracts dust.

### Colour

Amber reddens and deepens over years through surface oxidation, which is the basis of aged 老蜜蜡 tone. Carried further, the same reaction produces crazing and opacity.

This is the material's defining tension: **the process you want and the degradation you don't are the same reaction.** You are managing rate, not direction. Everywhere else in this document, oxidation is simply the goal.

## When you can wear things

**Yabai.** Not before it has a patina — the first of the ten prohibitions, because hand oil and moisture carry dirt into open pores where it will not come out. Short wear stretches from about two weeks, once the drag appears. Proper 醒珠 wearing weeks after roughly a month, when the surface has gone bright. Normal unrestricted wear at two to three months. Not for long stretches in hot weather until the patina is thick.

**Huanghuali.** Permitted earlier in the sequence — after the glove phase and the rest week, so roughly three to four weeks in. With conditions: avoid water, sun, wind and heavy sweat, and glove-work it for a while each time you take it off. Fully stable at about six months.

**Both at once — no.** Not on the same wrist. Huanghuali guides warn specifically against knocks against hard objects, and two dense hardwood strands grinding together all day will dent and scratch both; on huanghuali that cuts straight through the figure you are protecting. Yabai resin will also transfer scent onto the huanghuali. Separate wrists, or alternate days — which suits the cycles anyway.

## Cross-cutting rules

- **Cover the whole bead, especially the holes.** Every source that discusses technique singles out 孔口. Missing it shows later as a ring of lighter material.
- **Both hands, whole strand.** Not one-handed on one section.
- **Never rush.** 欲速则不达 — "haste does not arrive." The most-repeated warning across all these sources.
- **Passive wrist-wear is not 盘玩.** It is contact without the deliberate friction that does the work, and it brings sweat exposure. Wearing has its place in the cycle, but it is not a substitute for sessions.
- **Cord.** Elastic degrades from sweat and fails without warning. Restring roughly annually with daily wear; check every couple of months. If a strand will be **counted** rather than just worn, get it off elastic entirely — counting stresses cord far more than wearing does. Knotted cord with proper finishing.
- **Climate.** Dry heated interiors in winter and hot summers are both stressors. Humidity swings crack olive pits and can check wood.

## Failure modes

| Symptom | Cause | Fixable? |
|---|---|---|
| **盘黑** — flat muddy grey-black, grain killed | Sweat on unprotected wood | No |
| **盘花** — blotchy uneven colour | Uneven sweat contact, partial coverage | Largely no |
| **黏糊糊** — tacky, will not rub smooth | High-oil wood + bare hands too early | Yes: gloves, gentle work, rest |
| Uneven patina thickness | Air exposure before oil had emerged | Partially |
| Scent faded | Over-handling | No |
| Ring of light colour at bead holes | Missed 孔口 during sessions | Slowly |
| Grooves gone black on textured seeds | No brushing | Difficult |
| Jadeite yellowed and crazed | B货 handled or oiled | No |
| **退油** — blotches on amber | Abandoned the cycle halfway | No |
| Amber dry, dull, crazed | Sun or heat exposure | No |
| Amber scratched | Stiff brush, blade, or storage next to harder stones | No |
| Gourd mouldy | Played before fully cured | No |
| Gourd colour patchy | Outer waxy skin not removed first | Difficult |

**Good patina versus 盘黑:** good patina is warm, reddish, translucent, glossy. 盘黑 is flat, grey, opaque. They are easy to tell apart once either starts.

## Evidence, inference, and folklore

Worth keeping separate.

**Well-supported:**

- Low-oil material does not patina. Directly observed, consistently reported.
- Sealing produces white frost on high-oil yabai. Has a real physical mechanism.
- Sweat causes permanent blackening and blotching on light woods and 星月菩提.
- Handling reduces yabai's scent. Stated plainly even by sellers, who have no incentive to say it.
- Factory wax blocks the surface and should be removed first. Consistent across materials.

**Reasonable inference, not directly evidenced:**

- The sebum-polymerisation account of walnut and jade patina. Chemically plausible — squalene in skin oil oxidises and cross-links readily — but nobody has actually analysed a walnut patina.
- Airing a bracelet before sealing it. Not in the Chinese sources, which seal immediately after a gloved session. But their session is short gloved rubbing with clean dry hands, not a day on a sweaty wrist, so the extrapolation seems sound.

**Contested between sources:**

- Session frequency. Daily 20–30 minutes versus one session every two to seven days. Both are documented as working.
- Storage ventilation for yabai. One major site lists ventilated places among environments to avoid; another recommends a cool airy spot.

**Folklore:**

- 人养玉三年，玉养人一生 — "three years a person nourishes the jade, a lifetime the jade nourishes the person." A saying, not a mechanism. Jade absorbs nothing from you. The change is optical — oil filling micro-topography raises apparent translucency and gloss. Real and visible, but surface physics, not exchange.
- Jade cracking to protect its wearer.
- Precise patina timelines quoted to the month. Rate depends on material grade, oil content, skin chemistry, and climate.

**Marketing to ignore:**

- Specific percentage claims about resin content in yabai. Essential oil yields from *Platycladus orientalis* are well under 2% by extraction; "oiliness" in collecting is a subjective grading term for aged 陈化料, not a measured figure.
- Manufacturer claims that one glove material produces a measurably better patina than another after a set number of days. Invented copy with no method behind it.
- Steaming, boiling, or frying beads to accelerate anything.

## Quick reference

**Yabai:** sealed → gloved 20–30 min every 2 days → drag appears at 1–2 weeks, rest until gone → at 1 month open the bag, air 15–30 days → wear a week without rubbing → repeat 5–6×. Reduce frequency to protect scent.

**Huanghuali:** slit-bag 3–4 days → glove-clean 10–20 min/day → hang 2 weeks → gloved sessions, cotton bag between → bare hands → drag at 1–2 weeks → rest a week → repeat. ~6 months.

**Zitan:** bare hands from the start, light rest between sessions, dry hands, no water. 3–6 months.

**Chenxiang:** wear intermittently, damp-cloth wipe, shade dry, sealed storage. No rubbing ever.

**Seeds and walnuts:** bare hands, brush the textured ones, play/rest cycling, patience.

**Jade:** warm-water wash off the wax, then bare hands 1–2 hours daily, brush for dust on carved pieces, no rest cycle. The forgiving one.

**Amber:** bare hands, play until the drag appears (~10 days), dry it, repeat. No brush, no water, no heat, no solvents. Do not start unless you will finish.

**Gourds:** cure fully dry, remove the waxy skin, then bare hands and patience. Skin quality sets the ceiling.
