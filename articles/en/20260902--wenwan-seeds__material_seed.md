# Wenwan seeds (菩提子) — moon-and-stars, vajra, phoenix-eye and olive pit

Start with the awkward part. **The word 菩提子 (pútízǐ) almost never means what it promises.**

The bodhi tree is *Ficus religiosa*, the sacred fig the Buddha is said to have sat beneath. Its fruit are small figs, and nobody makes malas from them. The "bodhi seeds" sold on the Chinese wenwan market are the fruit of a dozen botanically unrelated plants: a rattan palm, a Nepali jujube, a tree in the Elaeocarpaceae, a Chinese olive. What they share is a purpose, not a lineage.

The name stuck by Buddhist logic rather than botanical: 菩提 is *bodhi*, "awakening," and any seed fit for a mala got called one. Dealers do not go out of their way to correct this. Knowing it is not pedantry — it lets you read listings honestly. "Genuine bodhi tree" on a product page is either an error or a sales line.

Below are the four types that actually carry the market.

**TL;DR:** seeds have no oil of their own. Everything that darkens and reddens them comes off your hands — so bare hands are mandatory, and gloves defeat the purpose. The mechanics are covered in the [pánwán care guide](/wiki/wenwan-care-guide).

## Xingyue 星月菩提 — "moon and stars"

**What it actually is:** the fruit of 黄藤 (huáng téng), a climbing rattan palm. Botanists long placed it in *Daemonorops* (*D. jenkinsiana*); it is now assigned to *Calamus*. Production centres on Hainan.

The name describes the surface literally. Small dark dots are scattered across the bead — the **"stars"**, marks left by seed-coat remnants in the indentations of the endosperm. One larger dot is the **"moon"**: the germ hole, 芽眼 (yá yǎn).

### How they are graded

**By the moon's position:**

| Type | Chinese | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Full moon | 正月 zhèng yuè | The moon sits dead centre on the bead's face. The prized version — a strung mala looks orderly |
| Crescent moon | 弦月 xián yuè | The moon is off-centre, typically at about 45° to the axis. Cheaper |

**By processing — and this one matters in practice.** The term **顺白 (shùn bái)** is usually translated "even white" and often presented as a natural grade of material. It is in fact the result of treatment: the seeds are **boiled to drive out their oil**, after which they come out whiter and more uniform in tone.

That carries a practical consequence dealers rarely mention: a de-oiled bead starts from a cleaner white base, so **the eventual patina will have more contrast** — but it also takes longer to work, because the oil it had has already been removed. A darker, untreated bead patinas faster, but will never give you the white → honey → wine-red progression.

Two more terms you will meet in listings: **阴皮 (yīn pí)**, patches of darkened "shadow" skin that some treat as a flaw and others as character; and **元宝籽 (yuánbǎo zǐ)**, seeds of a flattened shape resembling a sycee ingot.

### What to look for

Density decides everything. A spongy bead patinas sluggishly and cracks easily. Judge it by weight in the hand and by the stars: the finer and denser the speckling, the denser the material.

### The care quirk

Xingyue is the only one of the four that is **harmed by sweat.** It produces permanent blotching (盘花). So the standard here is not to start bare-handed at all: lay down a base polish with cloth or a cotton glove first, and only then move to hands.

## Jingang 金刚菩提 — "vajra"

**What it actually is:** the stone of *Elaeocarpus ganitrus*. The Indian tradition knows it as **rudraksha**, "the tears of Shiva"; the Chinese wenwan market arrived at the same material by its own route, through Buddhist prayer beads. The main sources are Nepal and Indonesia.

The surface is deeply cut with winding ridges — hence "vajra," the diamond sceptre.

### 瓣 — the lobes

The main grading parameter. The stone is divided into chambers, and the count shows on the outside as longitudinal lobes (瓣, bàn) — from two to more than ten, following the number of carpels.

- **五瓣 (five lobes)** — the standard, and the overwhelming majority of the market
- **六瓣 (six)** — noticeably more expensive already
- **一瓣, 二瓣 and the many-lobed** — rare, and precisely where both the collector interest and the fakes are concentrated

### Nepal versus Indonesia

| | Nepali | Indonesian |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Larger, typically 18–22 mm and up | Smaller, often 8–14 mm |
| Relief | Deep, sharp, "meaty" | Shallower, smoother |
| Density | High | Very high for the small size |
| Usually made into | Large bracelets, single beads | Long 108-bead strands |

### The care quirk

**Jingang is the case the brush exists for.** Grime packs into the grooves, and without regular brushing the relief goes black — one of the few seed mistakes that is genuinely hard to undo. A glove does not enter the grooves at all: it polishes the raised ridges, which are exactly the parts your hands already reach.

New stones normally get a brush-only phase before hands are allowed near them.

## Fengyan 凤眼菩提 — "phoenix eye"

**What it actually is:** the stone of *Ziziphus budhensis*, a jujube species endemic to the Temal region of Bagmati Province, Nepal, and described botanically only relatively recently. Stones of other *Ziziphus* species — including *Z. jujuba* var. *spinosa* — are sold under the same trade name, so "phoenix eye" on a label does not pin down the species.

In Nepal and Tibet it is made into bodhichitta malas; in China it entered wenwan as one of the most comfortable seeds for daily counting.

The name comes from the elongated mark on the side of the stone, which does look like a narrowed eye.

### Grading

The main parameter is **size**, in millimetres, and the logic here is inverted: **smaller is worth more.** 小凤眼 (xiǎo fèngyǎn), "small phoenix eye," at roughly 8–10 mm is the most expensive format, because a small stone is denser and far harder to match into an even strand. Large stones of 14 mm and up cost less.

After that, look at the eye itself — even, symmetrical, not broken — and at the consistency of the strand: 108 stones of one size with the eye in the same place is real matching work, and the price reflects it.

### The care quirk

Essentially none, which is why people love it. Fengyan is dense, tolerant of sweat, and reddens well and evenly. Of the four it is the best choice for someone who just wants to wear and count without running a regime. The surface is mostly smooth, so a glove works as a preparatory tool and a brush is needed only for dust.

Colour moves from pale yellow to a deep red-brown; with daily handling, over a year to eighteen months.

## Ganlanhe 橄榄核 — olive pit

**What it actually is:** the pit of the **Chinese olive**, *Canarium album* (青橄榄) — a plant unrelated to the Mediterranean olive. It is grown in Fujian, Guangdong, Guangxi, Hainan and Yunnan.

This is the only material on the list whose main tradition is not malas but **carving, 核雕 (hé diāo)**. Miniature pit carving is an old craft with its own canon; the classic text "Record of the Boat Carved from a Pit" (核舟记) describes a boat cut into a single olive pit, complete with passengers, windows and an inscription. The centre of the trade today is the Suzhou area.

### Grading

**By ridge count ("花")**, which corresponds to the internal chambers:

| Type | Ridges | How common |
|---|---|---|
| 二花 | 2 | Flattish, awkward to carve |
| 三花 | 3 | The ordinary kind, the bulk of the market |
| 四花 | 4 | Noticeably rarer |
| 五花 | 5 | A handful found per season |
| 多花 | 6+ | Rare |

**By raw-material type** there are another dozen or so categories — 铁圆核 "iron round," 油核 "oil pit," 奶油核 "cream pit," 水晶核 "crystal pit," 黄胖核 "yellow fat pit" (eastern Guangdong, dense yellow flesh), 牛头榄 "ox-head olive." They differ in density, wall thickness, and how the material behaves under the knife.

### The care quirk

Olive pit **cracks from humidity swings**, and this is not a theoretical risk — it is the most common way one gets ruined. Dry winter heating and a sharp move from cold street into a warm room are both dangerous. Keep it away from radiators, out of the sun, and in a closed box or pouch when you are not wearing it during a dry winter.

Carved pieces additionally need a soft brush: dust collects in the relief and is hard to get out later.

Colour heads toward a deep cherry red, with translucency in strong light on good material.

## How to work them

Briefly: **bare hands, and nothing else.** Seeds have no oil of their own, so all the patina feedstock comes off your skin. A glove here does not protect anything — it just cuts the process off from its supply.

Worth remembering:

- **The brush is not an alternative to hands but a different tool.** Hands polish smooth surfaces; the brush clears relief. On jingang and [walnuts](/wiki/wenwan-walnuts) it is indispensable; on fengyan it is only for dust.
- **Alternate work and rest** — 七盘七放. Rubbing only delivers oil to the surface; oxidation turns it into a film, and that needs time without friction.
- **Cover the whole bead, including around the holes.** A missed 孔口 shows up later as a lighter ring.
- **Xingyue is the sweat exception:** it needs a preparatory phase with cloth or a glove.
- Timeline: one to two years of regular work for stable colour. Figures quoted to the month are marketing — rate depends on the density of the material, your skin chemistry, and your climate.

The full account of mechanism, tools, cycles and failure modes is in [Pánwán (盘玩): how to work wenwan](/wiki/wenwan-care-guide).

## What else gets sold as 菩提子

The list is open-ended and trade names are invented freely. The ones you will meet most often are 太阳子 ("sun seed"), 千眼菩提 ("thousand-eye," a palm fruit), 白玉菩提 ("white jade bodhi") and 龙眼菩提 ("dragon eye"). Botanically they are strangers to each other; what unites them is what unites the four above — they can be strung and played.
